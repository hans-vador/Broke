# Mascot pipeline

Everything in `Broke/MascotLottie/` is generated by `rig2.py`. Don't hand-edit
the rigs — change the tool and re-run:

    python3 rig2.py

## The one thing to understand

**The mascots come from a single finished render.** Parts are only ever cut out
of that render, never sourced separately.

An earlier version of this pipeline composited separate arm and leg PNGs behind
a body PNG. That approach cannot be made to work, and a lot of time was burned
discovering it. The limb and the body are different renders, so there is no
continuous surface and no contact shading between them — the limb simply
disappears behind a hard silhouette edge. It reads as "stuck on" no matter how
carefully the joints are placed, and every fix (longer limbs, deeper tucks,
underside-anchored hips) only moved the problem around.

The source art in `Broke/Assets.xcassets/clay_*.imageset/` is a complete sculpted
character with limbs already attached, correctly shaded and correctly
proportioned. Those files are copied to
`Broke/MascotLottie/images/<fruit>_char.png`, which is then split into a torso
and two arms (see below) — all from those same pixels.

The retired layered rig, its generator and its limb art are kept for reference in
`MascotArt/retired_layered_rig/`. Don't revive it.

## Do not retouch the source art

`Broke/MascotLottie/images/<fruit>_char.png` is a straight copy of
`Broke/Assets.xcassets/clay_*.imageset/*.png`. Keep it that way.

Some renders do have small imperfections — apple, orange, blueberry and peach
have their legs sitting a few pixels clear of the body, and blueberry and peach
meet it end-on rather than flaring into it. Three separate tools were written to
correct that in image space (welding the gap, filleting the join, raising the
legs). **Every one of them made the characters look worse than leaving them
alone** — flat extruded pegs, dark smears from sampling shadow, visible dark
wedges at the leg tops, ghost bands from dragged pixels. They are parked in
`MascotArt/retired_layered_rig/dont_edit_the_art/` with a fuller write-up.

The imperfections are essentially invisible at the size these render. The
artifacts were not. These are finished renders with baked lighting; pixel
surgery will not improve on them.

If a character genuinely needs different geometry, **regenerate the art** — that
is what was eventually done for blueberry and peach, whose legs met the body
end-on. See `MascotArt/regenerated/` for the prompts and, more importantly, for
the two gates every regenerated character has to pass before it is accepted:
the defect must actually be fixed, and the character must not have drifted
off-model (silhouette IoU against the original, mean colour, bbox, area).

## Arms: cut from the same render, then rotated

The characters aren't animated as one flat sprite — the **arms are split out**
by `cutarms.swift` so they can trail the body, which is what reads as natural.
This is NOT a return to the retired layered rig: there the limbs were a
*different* render pasted behind a body, so the seam never matched. Here every
pixel comes from the same image. `verify.swift` composites the parts back at
rest and checks they reproduce the original — expect well under 0.5% mismatch,
all of it edge antialiasing.

Three rules make the seam invisible, and all three were learned the hard way:

1. **The torso keeps its own smooth silhouette.** Cutting the arm *out* of the
   torso leaves a notch, and a notch is a hole that opens the instant the arm
   moves. `fitEdge()` recovers the body's natural outline through the arm band
   by robustly fitting the outer edge and rejecting rows the arm pushes outward.
2. **Arm material must stay `safeMargin` inboard of the torso edge** wherever it
   isn't legitimately protruding — rotation drives anything near that edge
   straight through it, showing as a hard tab or a spike. Between the arm's root
   and the torso edge there's a strip with no arm material at all; that's fine,
   the torso is opaque there, so rotating can only reveal torso, never a hole.
3. `safeMargin` must exceed the worst-case swing, `rootRadius * sin(maxRotation)`.
   Tapering the root to nothing at its ends does the opposite of what you'd hope
   — it is thinnest exactly where the swing is largest.

Arm motion is **drag**: `arm_layers()` samples the body's vertical position from
`ARM_LAG` frames earlier, so the arms hang back as the body rises and swing
through as it lands. Arms moving in lockstep with the body look glued on. Legs
are deliberately left welded to the torso — they merge cleanly in the source art
and are load-bearing, so there's nothing to gain and a seam to lose.

## Where the rest of the life comes from

Beyond the arm drag, the body itself carries most of the performance. Four
things do almost all the work, in `rig2.py`:

* **Anchor at the feet.** `Char.anchor` is the bottom-centre of the character's
  bounding box, i.e. its ground contact point. Squash then presses the character
  down onto the floor and stretch pulls it up off the floor. Anchoring at the
  image centre — the obvious choice — just looks like the sprite is being scaled.
* **Squash and stretch**, roughly volume-preserving: x widens as y shortens.
  Because the anchor is at the feet, a positive scaleY grows the character
  *upward*, so peak height is `dy` **plus** the stretch. `headroom()` checks this
  fits on canvas and `main()` prints it per fruit — heed it; clipping the top of
  a jump looks terrible.
* **Asymmetric timing.** Each keyframe carries its own ease (`snap` for impacts,
  `float` for leaving the ground, `drop` for accelerating into a fall, `hold` for
  settling). Uniform easing everywhere is the single biggest reason mascot
  animation reads as dead.
* **A ground shadow** that shrinks and fades as the character lifts, and spreads
  as it squashes. Without it a bob looks like drifting in space rather than
  pushing off a floor. `shadow_layers()` derives it from the same beat table.

Per-fruit rhythm lives in `PERSONA` — the lemon jitters, the watermelon commits
to one heavy hop, the pear sways from the hip, the peach falls asleep.

## Eyelids and overlays use Lottie parenting

Blinks, squints, blush and the tongue are shape layers with `parent: CHAR_IND`,
positioned in the character's **image** coordinates. Parenting makes them inherit
every bit of squash, stretch and lean for free. Baking their transforms
separately makes them slide off the face the moment the body deforms. Layer
parenting is confirmed working in lottie-ios 4.6.1.

## Constraints

* lottie-ios 4.6.1 **cannot decode nested precomps.** Top-level image and shape
  layers only.
* Only the `el`, `sr` and `fl` shape primitives are used; those are known-good.
* Layer order is front-to-back — earlier in the array draws on top. Order is
  effects, eyelids, torso, arms, shadow — arms sit *behind* the torso so their
  cut edge stays buried.

## Checking the motion

Do **not** judge animation by spacing `xcrun simctl io screenshot` calls — each
blocks for about a second, so samples alias and a hopping character can look
perfectly still. Record instead, then pull evenly spaced frames:

    xcrun simctl io <udid> recordVideo --codec h264 --force rec.mov &
    # ...launch with SIMCTL_CHILD_MASCOT_FRUIT=<fruit> MASCOT_PREVIEW=<state>...
    swiftc -O frames.swift -o frames && ./frames rec.mov outdir 8 0.6 2.4

For one-shot clips (`celebrate`) start recording *before* launching the app, or
it is over before the recording begins.

## Other tools here

`measure.swift` emits `chars.json` (bbox, base colour, auto-detected eye AND
mouth positions, silhouette and underside profiles) for whatever PNGs you pass
it. The tongue is positioned from the detected **mouth**, not from eye spacing —
the faces aren't all proportioned alike, and deriving it from the eyes put the
lemon's tongue well below its mouth.
`profile.swift` prints a per-row width profile with run counts — useful for
comparing proportions against reference art numerically instead of by eye.
`recolor.swift`, `pad.swift`, `destub2.swift` and `lean.swift` are leftovers from
the retired pipeline, kept because they're generally useful image utilities.
